01  ·  The problem

Skills went volatile.

Skills are human capital — and AI has made that capital volatile: what you learn can depreciate in months, not decades.

  • The market prices job titles, not the skills underneath them — and no one measures the risk: how fast a portfolio of skills is going obsolete.
  • A student picks a program on the perceived prestige of a school, not on how aligned it is to real labour-market demand — the same blind spot for institutions, employers and funders.
  • The gap is not the raw data — postings and salaries exist. It is the infrastructure that turns them into a live, independent signal of demand and obsolescence risk.

04  ·  Why now

The window just opened.

  1. 1Volatility is now perceived. Skill lifetimes compressed from a decade to a few years. (Shiller: instruments become liquid when volatility is high and expected to stay high.)
  2. 2Public taxonomies matured. ESCO, O*NET and ISCO now give a shared, machine-usable vocabulary for skills.
  3. 3Regulatory convergence. The EU Pay Transparency Directive thickens salary data over time — the differentiating layer.
  4. 4Open-weight AI. An auditable, reproducible index is now buildable at low cost — a requirement for a reference that must stay recomputable.

05  ·  Market & incumbents

The signal doesn't exist in Europe.

  • In the US, retrospective analytics exist — Lightcast (~$100M revenue), LinkedIn, Mercer — proof the category has real demand.
  • In Europe it is largely absent: Lightcast isn't present, QS works one level up (country + occupation, survey-based), nobody reads skill/tool-level demand and obsolescence risk live.
  • These players are evidence of demand, not competitors here. Structural lock-out: whoever is paid by the rated can't publish an independent signal on them.
High resolution + independent = the empty quadrant
